# Academic vocabulary: the Academic Word List

The Academic Word List (AWL), compiled by Averil Coxhead (2000), contains 570 word families that occur frequently across academic texts in many disciplines but are not among the most common general English words. It is divided into 10 sublists by frequency: sublist 1 holds the most frequent families, sublist 10 the least frequent.

Online lists (with dictionary entries, pronunciation and example sentences):
- Oxford Learner's Dictionaries, Academic Word List (American English), sublists 1–10: https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/wordlist/american_english/academic/sublist01/ (change `sublist01` to `sublist02` … `sublist10`)
- Background on the list: https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/about/academic

The full list is not reproduced here; use the links above.

## 1. What the list is for

- **Precision:** academic words name processes and relations exactly (*derive*, *estimate*, *indicate*, *constitute*, *assume*), where general words are vague (*get*, *guess*, *show*, *make up*, *think*).
- **Reading and paraphrasing:** knowing these families helps to read papers quickly and to paraphrase sources in different words without changing the meaning.
- **Word families:** each entry covers related forms. Use the right form for the sentence: *analyse/analyze* (verb), *analysis* (noun), *analytical* (adjective), *analytically* (adverb).

Sublist 1 contains families that appear in almost every research article, for example: analyse, approach, area, assess, assume, authority, available, benefit, concept, consist, constitute, context, create, data, define, derive, distribute, economy, environment, establish, estimate, evident, factor, formula, function, identify, income, indicate, interpret, involve, issue, method, occur, percent, period, policy, principle, proceed, process, require, research, respond, role, section, significant, similar, source, specific, structure, theory, vary.

## 2. How to use it when writing

- Choose the academic word when it is more precise, not to sound formal. "The data indicate …" is more exact than "The data show …" only if you mean a tentative conclusion.
- Check the collocations: words have usual partners (*conduct an experiment*, *derive an equation*, *estimate a parameter*, *establish a relationship*, *a significant difference*). The Oxford Collocations Dictionary helps here.
- Use *significant* only for statistical significance or a stated threshold; otherwise write *large*, *marked*, or give the number.
- Use *data* as a plural noun in most journals ("the data were").
- Keep the same word for the same concept throughout the paper.
- Do not inflate: academic vocabulary is not the same as the decorative words listed in `avoid-ai-signs.md` §3 (pivotal, underscore, foster, leverage, intricate). Several of those also appear in academic word lists; use them only in their literal meaning.

## 3. Common general → academic replacements

| General | Academic (choose by meaning) |
|---|---|
| get | obtain, acquire, derive |
| find out | determine, identify, establish |
| show | indicate, demonstrate, illustrate |
| look at | examine, analyse, investigate |
| go up / go down | increase / decrease, rise / decline |
| think | assume, hypothesize, consider |
| guess | estimate, approximate |
| make up | constitute, comprise |
| about | approximately (numbers), regarding (topics) |
| big / small | large / small, substantial / minor |
| a lot of | many, a large number of, considerable |
| enough | sufficient, adequate |
| need | require |
| keep | maintain, retain |
| set up | establish, construct |
| check | verify, confirm, validate |

Plain words remain correct when they are precise: "used", "has", "is", "measured" are better than "utilized", "boasts", "serves as", "quantified the measurement of".

## 4. Practising with the list

- When reading a paper from your repository, note sublist words and the phrases around them (for example "*was derived from*", "*is consistent with*").
- When revising, look for vague general verbs in your draft (get, show, look at, make) and replace them where a more exact word exists.
- The Oxford Text Checker (https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/text-checker/) shows which words in a text are included in Oxford's word lists.
