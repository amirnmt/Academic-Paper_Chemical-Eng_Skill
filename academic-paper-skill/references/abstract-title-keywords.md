# Abstract, title, keywords, highlights and graphical abstract

These parts are read by far more people than the paper itself. Many readers, and the editor, form their opinion here. Write them last, from the finished paper.

## 1. Abstract

### 1.1 Rules
- Self-contained: understandable without the paper. It is reproduced alone in databases.
- No references (exception: a paper that directly replies to another), no figure or table citations, no undefined abbreviations.
- No statement or conclusion that is not in the paper.
- Concentrate on results, not on a list of what was done.
- No evaluative or judgemental remarks ("surprisingly, …").
- Within the word limit (often 150–300 words); using fewer words is fine.
- Paraphrase rather than paste sentences from the paper.
- Avoid empty endings: "The results are discussed", "More research is needed".

### 1.2 Components
1. Background, aim, problem, what the paper does.
2. Methodology or materials.
3. Results; achievement or contribution; implications.
4. Applications.
5. Limitations or future work (occasionally).

Two common forms:
- **Structured abstract** (or journals with headings): components 1–4 appear separately, in roughly this order.
- **Compact abstract**: 2–3 components only, often combined in one sentence (e.g. what the paper does + method + aim). Order is flexible, but methods come before results. It usually includes results or contribution and often the method.

Engineering abstracts typically state background, purpose, methods, results and conclusion. Chemical engineering abstracts emphasize the methods; the purpose is stated less often, so stating it clearly is an advantage.

### 1.3 Word budget (for about 250 words)
- Background and gap: 40–50 words (1–2 sentences).
- Aim and approach: 30–40 words.
- Methods: 40–50 words.
- Results with numbers: 80–100 words (primary result first).
- Contribution or implication: 30–40 words.

### 1.4 Tense by component
- Gap or problem: present simple ("However, these correlations are not valid when …").
- What the paper does: present simple ("This paper presents …"; "We compare …").
- What was done: past simple ("Five correlations were evaluated …"); present simple is also used for calculations in the paper ("The model is validated against …").
- Results: past simple (more common) or present simple if you present them as established ("The model consistently underpredicts …").
- Implications: present simple, often with a modal ("The results indicate that …"; "This approach can be used to …").

### 1.5 Skeleton (placeholders)
> [Process/system] is [importance]. [Current models/methods] rely on [assumption/limitation], which [consequence]. This study [aim] by [approach]. [Method details: model, data, validation]. [Main result with number]. [Second result with number]. [Comparison or validation metric]. These results [implication for design, operation or modelling].

## 2. Title

- Predict and describe the content accurately; reflect the scope (a case study vs a general survey).
- Start from the research question: "How does X affect Y?" → "Effect of X on Y in …"; "What is the difference between X and Y?" → "Comparison of X and Y for …".
- Name the main factor, the system and, where possible, the finding; put the most important element first.
- Include the key terms people search for.
- Avoid filler ("A study of", "Investigation on", "Some aspects of", "Novel"), unexplained abbreviations and brand names.
- Check compound nouns for ambiguity: the last noun is the real noun; nouns before it modify it. Long noun stacks are hard to decode; use prepositions to break them.
- Prepositions change meaning: "with" can mean "using" or "having"; "evidence for" (supports) vs "evidence of" (observable sign).
- Sentence case unless the journal requires title case.
- Respect the journal's length limit; a short title is harder to write than a long one.
- Write a running title if required.

Examples (illustrative):
- Weak: "A study of heat transfer in methanol reactors"
- Better: "Wall heat-transfer correlations for low tube-to-particle diameter ratios: validation against pilot-scale temperature profiles in methanol synthesis"

## 3. Keywords

- Specific, searchable terms: methods, materials, processes, properties, systems.
- Do not repeat words already in the title (the title is already indexed); some journals forbid it.
- Avoid general words (study, analysis, experiment, change) and fashionable terms that return thousands of hits.
- Add a broader or a narrower term than the title (e.g. title has "methanol synthesis" → keyword "CO2 hydrogenation" or "packed-bed heat transfer").
- Stay within the journal's limit; fewer is allowed.

## 4. Highlights

- Usually 3–5 bullet points, each within a character limit (often 85 characters including spaces).
- Main results and contributions, not background.
- Concrete and specific: "Five wall heat-transfer correlations compared at low d_t/d_p" rather than "Heat transfer was studied".

## 5. Graphical abstract

- One image summarizing the main message: often the system, the method and the key result in one panel.
- Prefer a new, simple figure designed for the purpose; reuse a paper figure only if it already shows the main result clearly.
- Readable at small size; little text.

## 6. Checklist

- [ ] Abstract self-contained: no references, figures or undefined abbreviations; nothing not in the paper.
- [ ] Contains aim, method, main quantitative results, implication.
- [ ] Results take most of the words; primary result first.
- [ ] Tense matches each component.
- [ ] Title accurate, specific, searchable, without filler; compound nouns and prepositions checked.
- [ ] Keywords specific and not repeating title words.
- [ ] Highlights concrete and within the character limit.
- [ ] Objective wording consistent with Introduction and Conclusion.
