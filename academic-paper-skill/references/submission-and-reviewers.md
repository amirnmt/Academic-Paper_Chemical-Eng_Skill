# Revising, submitting and answering reviewers

## 1. Before writing: decisions

- **Target journal:** choose it before writing; scope, readership, article types, length limits and style all shape the paper. Read several recent papers in it and copy their structure.
- **Article type:** full research article, short communication, review. A review article has no Methods/Results structure; it is organized by themes and needs its own plan.
- **Authorship:** agree on authors and order early; everyone listed must have contributed substantially and approve the final version.
- **Data and code availability:** decide what will be shared and where.

## 2. Writing the first version

- Start where the material is clearest: usually Methods and Results (with figures and tables), then Discussion, Introduction, Conclusion, Abstract, Title.
- Build the reference list as you write; insert each citation immediately.
- Do not aim for a final version in the first draft. Get the content down, then revise.
- If you are stuck, write the figure captions and the paragraph that describes each figure; the Results often follow from them.

## 3. Revising your own draft

Leave the draft for a few days, then read it as a reader would. Read it aloud to find clumsy sentences. Revise in rounds, from large to small:
1. Structure and argument (does each section do its job; is the gap answered?).
2. Paragraphs (one idea each; topic sentences tell the argument).
3. Sentences and words (clarity, concision, terminology).
4. Final pass from beginning to end (consistency, numbers, references).

Check the eight Cs:
- **Compliance:** journal guidelines, template, word limits, reference style.
- **Completeness:** all required parts and information present.
- **Composition:** logical organization and flow.
- **Correctness:** facts, numbers, equations, references, spelling and grammar.
- **Clarity:** unambiguous words, explicit logic, defined terms.
- **Conciseness:** no unnecessary words, sentences or figures.
- **Consistency:** terms, symbols, units, number formats, abbreviations, spelling variant (British or American).
- **Courtesy:** fair to other authors, respectful language, easy for readers.

Use spell- and grammar-checkers, but do not rely on them. Ask a colleague who has not seen the work to read it.

## 4. Submission package

- Manuscript in the journal's template and format (line numbers and double spacing if required).
- Title page with authors, affiliations, corresponding author.
- Figures and tables in the required format and resolution.
- Highlights, graphical abstract, keywords if required.
- Supplementary material.
- Declarations: funding, conflicts of interest, data availability, author contributions (CRediT), use of AI tools.
- Cover letter.
- Suggested reviewers, if requested: experts without conflicts of interest (not co-authors, close colleagues or people at your institution).

## 5. Cover letter

Short and specific. State:
- the title and article type, and the journal;
- in two or three sentences, what the paper does and why it matters to this journal's readers;
- that the work is original, not published or under consideration elsewhere, and that all authors approved it;
- any prior presentation (e.g. a conference abstract), and any reviewers to exclude, with reasons;
- the corresponding author's contact details.

Template:
> Dear Editor,
>
> Please consider our manuscript "[Title]" for publication as a [research article] in [Journal].
>
> [One sentence on the problem.] [One sentence on what we did.] [One sentence on the main result and why it is relevant to the journal's readers.]
>
> This manuscript is original, has not been published, and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission and declare no conflicts of interest.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name, affiliation, email]

After submission, check that the journal acknowledged receipt.

## 6. Decisions

- **Accept:** submit the final version promptly.
- **Minor or major revision:** revise and resubmit within the deadline.
- **Reject and resubmit:** treat as a major revision; the editor will consider a new submission after substantial changes.
- **Reject:** do not argue unless there is a clear factual error. Revise using the reviewers' comments, reformat, and submit elsewhere. Many published papers were first rejected by another journal.
- Desk rejection (without review) is common in high-volume journals; it is rarely worth contesting.

Do not take criticism personally. Read the reports carefully, look past rude phrasing to the content, and wait a day before replying.

## 7. Revising after review

A revision request is not an obligation; decide whether the changes are worth it for this journal.

Avoid two extremes:
- **Zero acceptance:** arguing that every comment is wrong. Editors do not accept this.
- **Total acceptance:** changing everything without thought, even when comments contradict each other or are wrong. This does not make acceptance easier.

Good criticism says what is wrong, why, and how to improve it. Accept sound suggestions, including those that are not worse than your original. If a reviewer only expresses a preference without arguments, acknowledge the difference politely and explain why you keep your version.

When you disagree, support your position with published evidence (preferably peer-reviewed primary studies) or with additional analysis, not with opinion.

## 8. Response letter (point by point)

Make the editor's work easy: they must compare the original, the comments, your replies and the revised version.

Structure:
1. Short opening: thank the editor and reviewers; summarize the main changes.
2. For each reviewer, every comment in order:
   - **Comment** (quoted in full or summarized faithfully, numbered R1.1, R1.2 …),
   - **Response:** what you did and why,
   - **Change:** where in the revised manuscript (page/line, section), with the new text quoted if short.
3. Reply to every single comment, including the ones you do not act on.

Example:
> **R1.3** "The authors should justify the choice of the wall heat-transfer correlation."
>
> **Response:** We agree that this choice needed justification. We now compare five correlations (Section 2.4) and show in Figure 5 that the selected correlation gives the lowest error at the tube-to-particle ratio of our reactor.
>
> **Change:** Section 2.4, p. 7, lines 152–168; new Figure 5.

Disagreeing politely:
> **Response:** We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. We considered a two-dimensional heterogeneous model, but the Biot numbers of the pellets (< 0.1, Table 2) show that intraparticle temperature gradients are negligible; a pseudo-homogeneous model is therefore adequate, as also shown by [n]. We added this justification in Section 2.3 (p. 6, lines 130–134).

Tone: polite, factual, never sarcastic. Thank reviewers where they improved the paper, but do not overdo it.

Mark changes in the manuscript as the journal requests (track changes, coloured text, or a separate marked-up version).

## 9. Final version and after acceptance

- Check proofs carefully: equations, symbols, table alignment, figure quality, author names, affiliations, references.
- Answer the typesetter's queries; change nothing substantial at proof stage.
- After publication, share the paper (repository, preprint server where allowed), and update data and code links.
