# Discussion and conclusion: interpreting findings with the right strength

The Discussion answers one question: what do the data mean? It is the hardest section to write and the most common reason for rejection. Interpret, compare, explain significance, and state limitations, at exactly the strength the evidence supports.

## 1. Structure (a menu; select and order for your paper)

1. **Revisit previous sections.** Restate the main findings briefly, in the same words as the objective. Do not repeat the Results.
2. **Map the findings onto existing research.** Agree, disagree, extend. When results differ from others, give specific, plausible reasons: conditions, materials, model assumptions, scale, data quality.
3. **Explain mechanisms.** Why the trends occur: kinetics, heat or mass-transfer limitation, thermodynamics, deactivation, numerical artefacts.
4. **Achievement and contribution.** What is new; whether the hypothesis is supported; whether the gap from the Introduction is closed; principles, relationships and generalizations that follow from the results; the new picture, if there is one.
5. **Refine the implications.** Theoretical and practical: design, operation, scale-up, safety, control, method choice.
6. **Limitations.** State them honestly; where justified, explain why their effect on the conclusions is small.
7. **Current and future work.** Specific and justified by the limitations or by new observations.

**Concluding moves in engineering articles:** state a specific outcome → interpret it → state its significance → contrast it with previous outcomes → state its limitations; then state research implications and point to further research. Chemical engineering papers often end with a section headed only "Conclusion(s)" that states the main results and their significance.

## 2. Comparing with previous studies

For each comparison:
1. State your result briefly.
2. Name the study and its result.
3. Say whether they agree, and by how much.
4. If they differ, give the most likely reason, and say how confident you are.
5. Say what the comparison means for the field.

Agreement:
- "This trend is consistent with the measurements of [12], who reported …"
- "The predicted hot-spot position agrees with [14] within [value] m."
- "These results confirm, for low tube-to-particle ratios, the finding of [9] that …"

Disagreement:
- "In contrast to [15], we observed no …. This difference may be attributed to the higher flow rates used in [15]."
- "The deviation from [8] is probably due to their assumption of …"
- Do not stay silent when results differ from previous findings; reviewers will notice.

## 3. Rules

Do:
- Present principles, relationships and generalizations that follow from the results.
- Refer back to the central problem; say what was not found or not supported.
- Point out gaps or inconsistencies that remain or that your results reveal.
- Explain the significance of the results; avoid leaving the reader to ask "so what?".
- Summarize the evidence for each conclusion. Do not leave the inference to the reader.
- Discuss theoretical and practical implications.

Do not:
- Repeat the Results in other words.
- Introduce new results. If a result becomes relevant here, add it to Results and its method to Methods.
- Pretend to have solved everything.
- Discuss every speculative possibility.
- Over-interpret: a result from 25 samples or one reactor does not support a statement about all cases. Generalize only as far as the data reach.
- End with throwaway sentences ("more research is needed", "significant new avenues for future research", "the implications are discussed"). End with a precise, well-founded statement.

Proportion: a Discussion longer than about twice the Results usually discusses the findings to death.

Negative results from a well-designed study are a contribution: discuss which ideas they challenge, which earlier findings they question, and what they confirm.

## 4. Stance: what strong discussions do

A corpus study of 213 chemical engineering discussion sections found that discussions from high-impact papers differ from low-impact ones mainly in three ways:
- **Involvement and interaction:** clearer authorial presence (first person where the journal allows, attitude markers), and a balanced use of hedges and boosters.
- **Evaluation followed by explanation:** evaluative statements are immediately explained or elaborated ("This agreement is remarkable because …").
- **Explicit statements of results and claims:** authors state what they found and what it means, with confidence where the evidence is strong.

Translate this into checks:
- [ ] Each paragraph makes a clear claim in the authors' voice.
- [ ] Each evaluation ("good agreement", "significant improvement") is followed by the reason or the number.
- [ ] Hedges where the evidence is indirect; boosters (clearly, demonstrates, confirms) only where it is direct.
- [ ] No sentence is so hedged that it says nothing.

## 5. Hedging: matching the strength of the claim to the evidence

**Levels of certainty**
| Evidence | Wording |
|---|---|
| Direct evidence (measured, proven) | no modal: "The pressure drop was caused by catalyst fines." |
| Virtually certain, not proven | must / must have: "The deviation must have been caused by …" (signals certainty without proof) |
| Probable | should, is likely to, probably, it is likely that |
| Possible | may, might, could, can, possibly, perhaps, a possible explanation is |
| Unlikely | is unlikely to, it is improbable that |

**Modal verbs in the past and negative** (common source of errors)
- may/might have + past participle: possible past event. "The catalyst may have sintered."
- could not have: past event impossible. "Mass-transfer limitation could not have caused this, because …"
- must have: virtually certain past event. "The thermocouple must have shifted."
- should have: expected but not observed, or advisable but not done. "The profile should have flattened downstream."
- must not ≠ do not have to: "must not" forbids; "do not have to" means "not necessary".
- "should" has two meanings: likely ("The model should be valid up to …") and advisable ("Future work should …"). Make the meaning clear.

**Other hedging devices**
- Verbs: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, tend to.
- Adverbs: probably, possibly, generally, largely, partly, approximately.
- Limiting phrases: under the conditions studied, within the investigated range, for the present data, to some extent.
- Impersonal frames: it is possible that, there is evidence that, these results are consistent with.

**Do not hedge measured facts** ("the temperature may have increased by 12 K" when you measured it), and do not stack hedges ("this might possibly suggest").

**Boosters** (use only with direct evidence): clearly, demonstrates, confirms, establishes, shows, it is evident that.

## 6. Writing limitations well

Pattern: acknowledge → explain the effect → mitigate or bound it → turn it into future work.
> "The model assumes constant physical properties along the bed. Because the temperature varies by less than [value] K downstream of the hot spot, this assumption changes the predicted conversion by less than [value] % (sensitivity analysis in the Supplementary Material). It may, however, be significant at higher heat release, which will be examined in future work."

Listing limitations shows reviewers you know them and lets you explain why they do not overturn the conclusions.

## 7. Criticizing other work diplomatically

- Present the strength of your work instead of the weakness of others: "Our dataset covers [n] operating points, which allows …" rather than "[15] used too few data".
- Hedge criticism: "may have been underpowered", "did not account for", "was limited to".
- Preserve the original authors' emphasis when you paraphrase them.

## 8. The Conclusion section

- Answer the objective directly.
- Give the key quantitative findings (the 2–4 most important numbers).
- State the main limitation and the outlook.
- No new data, no new citations, no word-for-word repetition of the abstract.
- If there is no separate Conclusion, end the Discussion with a short concluding paragraph that does this job.
- End on a clear statement. A clear stream of discussion should not end in a "swampy delta".

## 9. Tense

- Established knowledge and other studies' accepted findings: present simple, with citation.
- Your specific results: past simple.
- Generalizations from your results that you defend: present simple.
- Interpretation: modal verbs.

## 10. Style

- Write simply; fancy language can disguise shallow thinking.
- Use your own words; do not recycle sentences from earlier sections.
- One idea per paragraph, opened by a topic sentence that states the claim.

## 11. Checklist

- [ ] Opens with the main findings in the objective's wording; no repetition of Results.
- [ ] Every main finding interpreted and compared with named studies.
- [ ] Disagreements explained with specific reasons.
- [ ] Mechanisms explained; claims supported by summarized evidence.
- [ ] Contribution and closure of the gap stated explicitly.
- [ ] Practical and theoretical implications given.
- [ ] Limitations acknowledged, bounded and linked to future work.
- [ ] Certainty of each claim matches the evidence; no overclaiming, no hedged facts.
- [ ] No new results; no throwaway ending; proportionate length.
