# Discussion and conclusion: interpreting findings with the right strength

The Discussion answers one question: **what do the data mean?** It is the hardest section to write and the most common reason for rejection, even when the data are valid and interesting. A faulty interpretation can hide the true meaning of good data. Many discussions are also too long: authors unsure of their facts or reasoning hide behind a cloud of words.

Use with `paragraphs-and-sentences.md`, `vocabulary-by-section.md` §6–7 and `phrasebank.md` (Discussion, Conclusion).

## 1. The Discussion mirrors the Introduction

The Introduction narrowed from the field to your question; the Discussion widens from your findings back to the field (an inverted funnel). What the Introduction opened, the Discussion closes:

| Introduction | Discussion |
|---|---|
| ends with the present work | starts by revisiting your work and its main findings |
| maps previous research | places your findings on that map |
| identifies a gap or problem | says to what extent the gap is closed or the problem solved |
| establishes importance | ends with wider implications and applications |

The Introduction poses questions, at least implicitly; the Discussion must answer them. A common fault is a Discussion that never returns to the questions the Introduction asked.

## 2. How to start

**First sentence:** restate the main finding, using the same key words as the objective (repeating the words creates an "echo" that reminds the reader of the question).
- "This study shows that wall heat-transfer correlations derived for large tube-to-particle ratios overpredict the hot-spot temperature at low ratios."
- "The comparison of five correlations against pilot-scale profiles identified one correlation that predicts the hot-spot temperature within 3 K."

Alternatives: revisit the aim or the problem briefly, then give the main finding; or begin with the prior work your study extends ("Previous studies established … [refs]. The present results extend this to …").

Avoid: repeating the results section in other words; introducing new results; starting with a limitation.

## 3. Components (a menu; select and order for your paper)

A common order (inverted funnel):
1. **Revisit:** restate the main findings briefly, in the words of the objective.
2. **Map onto the literature:** agree, disagree, extend. When results differ from others, give specific, plausible reasons (conditions, materials, assumptions, scale, data).
3. **Explain mechanisms:** why the trends occur (kinetics, heat or mass transfer limitation, thermodynamics, deactivation, numerical artefacts).
4. **Achievement and contribution:** what is new; whether the hypothesis is supported; whether the gap is closed; principles, relationships and generalizations that follow; the "new picture" if there is one.
5. **Implications:** theoretical and practical (design, operation, scale-up, safety, control, choice of method).
6. **Strengths and limitations:** substantial strengths (a superior technique, a large dataset, long operating period) and limitations (assumptions, data quality, range), each with its effect on the conclusions.
7. **Future work:** specific, justified by the limitations or new observations.
8. **Conclusion:** a short, clear statement of the significance of the work.

**Concluding moves in engineering articles** (move analysis of five fields): state a specific outcome → interpret it → state its significance → contrast it with previous outcomes → state its limitations; then state research implications and point to further research. Chemical engineering papers often end with a section headed only "Conclusion(s)" that states the main results and their significance.

## 4. Paragraph types and their first sentences

| Paragraph | First sentence pattern | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Main finding | "The main finding of this study is that …" | evidence from your results → what it means |
| Explanation | "The overprediction is most likely caused by …" | mechanism → supporting evidence → alternative explanations and why they are less likely |
| Agreement with literature | "These results are consistent with [12], who …" | what [12] found → how close the agreement is → what the agreement adds (e.g., extends to new conditions) |
| Disagreement | "In contrast to [15], we found no …" | what [15] found → most likely reason for the difference → how confident you are → what it means |
| Contribution | "These results provide the first …" | evidence → why it matters → for whom |
| Implication | "For reactor design, this means that …" | practical consequence → conditions under which it holds |
| Limitation | "One limitation of the present model is …" | effect on results → bound or mitigation → future work |
| Future work | "The next step is to …" / "Future work should …" | what exactly, and why |
| Conclusion | "In conclusion, …" / "This study has shown that …" | key numbers → significance → outlook |

## 5. Comparing with previous studies

For each comparison:
1. State your result briefly.
2. Name the study and its result.
3. Say whether they agree, and by how much.
4. If they differ, give the most likely reason and how confident you are.
5. Say what the comparison means for the field.

Choose names and studies carefully: readers use them to see how your work relates to and differs from existing research. A simple comparative is often the clearest way to show a difference: "more accurate than", "an order of magnitude faster than", "with a lower error than".

Disagreement must be addressed, not hidden. Point out exceptions, lack of correlation and unsettled points; never adjust or omit data that do not fit.

## 6. Rules

Do:
- Say what general relationships and principles the results reveal; interpret them instead of repeating them.
- Summarize the evidence for each conclusion; do not leave the inference to the reader.
- State conclusions as clearly as possible.
- Discuss theoretical implications and practical applications; avoid the reader's "so what?".
- Note substantial strengths and limitations yourself. Reviewers will notice limitations anyway; if you name them, you can also explain their effect.

Do not:
- Repeat the Results in other words or present new results (if a result becomes relevant here, add it to Results and its method to Methods).
- Extrapolate beyond your data: illuminate your area of truth well rather than claiming the whole truth, or even your well-supported conclusions will be doubted.
- Pretend to have solved everything, or discuss every speculative possibility.
- End with throwaway sentences ("more research is needed", "the implications are discussed", "this opens new avenues"). End on a precise, well-founded statement.

Use the first person where you mean it: "We conclude that …" is shorter and clearer than "It is concluded that …".

Proportion: a Discussion much longer than about twice the Results usually discusses the findings to death.

Negative results from a well-designed study are a contribution: say which ideas they challenge, which earlier findings they question, and what they confirm.

## 7. Stance: what strong discussions do

A corpus study of 213 chemical engineering discussion sections (Jin 2018) found that discussions from highly cited papers differ from those of rarely cited ones mainly in three ways:
- **Involvement:** a clearer authorial presence (first person where the journal allows, attitude markers) and a balanced use of hedges and boosters.
- **Evaluation followed by explanation:** evaluative statements are immediately explained or elaborated ("This agreement is notable because …").
- **Explicit claims:** authors state clearly what they found and what it means, confidently where the evidence is strong.

Checks:
- [ ] Each paragraph makes a clear claim in the authors' voice.
- [ ] Each evaluation ("good agreement", "a substantial improvement") is followed by the reason or the number.
- [ ] Hedges where evidence is indirect; boosters (clearly, demonstrates, confirms) only where it is direct.
- [ ] No sentence is so hedged that it says nothing.

## 8. Hedging: matching the strength of the claim to the evidence

**Certainty scale**
| Evidence | Wording |
|---|---|
| Direct evidence (measured, proven) | no modal: "The pressure drop was caused by catalyst fines." |
| Virtually certain, not proven | must / must have: "The deviation must have been caused by …" |
| Probable | should, is likely to, probably, it is likely that |
| Possible | may, might, could, can, possibly, perhaps, a possible explanation is |
| Virtually impossible | cannot / cannot have / could not have |

An everyday way to remember the scale: if a colleague usually needs 20 minutes to walk home and left 18 minutes ago, she *may* be home; 30 minutes ago, she *should* be home; 50 minutes ago, she *must* be home; 5 minutes ago, she *cannot* be home yet.

**Modal verbs: meanings and forms** (frequent source of errors)
- Possibility: may / might / could + verb; past: may have / might have / could have + past participle. Negative possibility: may not / might not (not "cannot", which means impossible). "may well" = stronger possibility; "might" is slightly weaker than "may".
- Probability or expectation: should; past: should have + past participle (expected but did not happen: "The profile should have flattened downstream"); should not have (happened, unexpectedly).
- Virtual certainty: must / must have (no other explanation possible); negative: cannot / cannot have / could not have.
- Ability: can / could; for a specific past occasion use "was able to"; for other tenses "is able to / will be able to".
- Necessity: must / need to / have to; not necessary: need not / does not need to / does not have to. "Did not need to heat" says nothing about whether you heated; "need not have heated" means you heated unnecessarily.
- "must not" means "not allowed", not "not necessary"; "should" means both "likely" and "advisable": make the meaning clear.

**Other hedging devices**
- Verbs: suggest, indicate, appear, seem, tend to.
- Adverbs: probably, possibly, likely, generally, largely, partly, approximately.
- Limiting phrases: under the conditions studied, within the investigated range, for this reactor, in most cases, to some extent.
- Impersonal frames: it is possible that, it is likely that, the evidence suggests that, these results are consistent with.
- Frequency or quantity qualifiers: often, in many cases, in some cases.

**Do not hedge measured facts** ("the temperature may have increased by 12 K" when you measured it), and do not stack hedges ("this might possibly suggest a tendency").

**Boosters** (only with direct evidence): clearly, demonstrates, confirms, establishes, it is evident that.

## 9. Writing limitations and future work

Pattern: acknowledge → explain the effect → bound or mitigate it → turn it into future work.
> "The model assumes constant physical properties along the bed. Because the temperature varies by less than [value] K downstream of the hot spot, this assumption changes the predicted conversion by less than [value] % (Supplementary Material, Fig. S4). It may, however, matter at higher heat release, which future work will examine."

Framing limitations as directions for future research shows that the field still has open questions, which supports the value of your work.

"Future work **will** …" or "we are currently …" states your own plan (and signals that you are working on it). "Future work **should** …" invites the research community. Choose deliberately.

## 10. Applications

When the work has a clear use, state it with a modal verb that matches your confidence: "The recommended correlation can be used for the design of low-ratio tubes"; "This approach could support operator decisions on coolant temperature"; "It should be possible to extend the method to …".

## 11. Criticizing other work diplomatically

- Present the strength of your work instead of the weakness of others: "Our dataset covers [n] operating points, which allows …" rather than "[15] used too few data".
- Hedge criticism: "may have been limited by", "did not account for", "was restricted to".
- "Did not examine" is acceptable; "failed to", "ignored", "overlooked" are not.
- Preserve the original authors' emphasis when you paraphrase them.

## 12. The Conclusion section

Some journals require a separate Conclusion(s) section; others end the Discussion with a concluding paragraph. Either way, usually one paragraph (or a short numbered list if the journal uses that style):
1. what was done (one sentence, in the words of the objective);
2. what was found (the 2–4 key quantitative findings);
3. what the findings mean (significance, implications);
4. the main limitation and the outlook (one sentence each).

Rules: no new data, no new citations, no word-for-word repetition of the abstract. Many readers read the conclusion first, so it must stand alone and carry the take-home message. End on a clear statement, not a fading "more research is needed".

Tense: present perfect for what the study achieved ("This study has shown …"), past for what was done and found, present for what the findings mean.

## 13. Worked example (structure annotated; content illustrative)

> [Revisit + main finding] This study shows that wall heat-transfer correlations derived for tube-to-particle ratios above 10 overpredict the hot-spot temperature of a low-ratio methanol synthesis tube by up to [value] K, whereas the correlation of [Author] predicts it within [value] K.
>
> [Explanation] The overprediction is most likely caused by the higher void fraction near the wall at low ratios, which increases local heat transfer [n]. The correlations that include a wall-region term [n,n] are the ones that agree best with the measurements, which supports this explanation.
>
> [Mapping] These results agree with the non-reacting experiments of [n], who reported a similar increase of the wall coefficient at low ratios. They extend this finding to reacting conditions, where the error in the coefficient translates directly into the predicted hot-spot temperature.
>
> [Implication] For reactor design, the choice of correlation therefore changes the predicted safe coolant temperature by about [value] K, which is comparable to the operating margin of industrial reactors.
>
> [Limitation → future work] The comparison is limited to one particle shape and one tube diameter. Future work will extend the measurements to [conditions].
>
> [Conclusion] The results show that correlation choice, not model dimensionality, dominates the uncertainty of hot-spot predictions at low tube-to-particle ratios.

(Values and citations are placeholders.)

## 14. Checklist

- [ ] Opens with the main findings in the objective's words; no repetition of Results; no new results.
- [ ] Answers the questions the Introduction posed; states to what extent the gap is closed.
- [ ] Every main finding interpreted and compared with named studies; disagreements explained.
- [ ] Mechanisms explained; evidence summarized for each conclusion.
- [ ] Contribution, implications and applications stated; no "so what?" left open.
- [ ] Strengths and limitations stated, with their effect on the conclusions; future work specific (will vs should chosen deliberately).
- [ ] Certainty of each claim matches the evidence; modal verbs used correctly; no stacked hedges, no hedged facts.
- [ ] First person used where it makes claims clearer; proportionate length.
- [ ] Ends on a clear, specific statement; the conclusion stands alone.
