# Academic style, paragraphs and grammar

Scientific style is plain: state facts as simply as possible. Clarity and precision make new information easy to understand; simple sentences often show deeper thinking than complicated ones.

## 1. Features of academic style

- **Evidence-based:** claims are supported by data or citations.
- **Precise and detailed:** numbers, conditions and specific terms instead of vague words.
- **Cautious where appropriate:** certainty matches evidence (see `discussion-hedging.md`).
- **Impersonal or controlled first person:** the focus is on the work; "we" is accepted in most engineering journals for decisions and claims.
- **Formal vocabulary:** words of classical origin are common (investigate, obtain, increase rather than look into, get, go up).
- **Nominalization:** processes expressed as nouns ("the reduction of the catalyst") make text compact, but stacking them makes it dense. Balance them with verbs.
- **No contractions** (do not, it is), **no rhetorical questions**, no clichés, no emotive or promotional words (revolutionary, amazing, obviously, very unique).
- **Unbiased, inclusive language.**

## 2. Paragraphs

**One idea per paragraph.** A new idea, a contrasting argument, or a new aspect of an idea starts a new paragraph.

**Components**
1. **Topic sentence** near the start: states the paragraph's point and how it connects to the main argument.
2. **Supporting sentences:** facts, data, citations, explanation, analysis.
3. **Closing sentence:** interpretation, or a link to the next paragraph.

**Qualities to check**
- Focus: the paragraph does not drift into other topics.
- Coherence: sentences follow logically; signalling words show the relations.
- Development: the idea is explained and supported; a two-sentence paragraph usually needs development or merging.

**Test:** read only the first sentence of each paragraph in a section. The sequence should tell the argument of the section.

## 3. Sentences

- One sentence, one main point; add at most one interpretive clause.
- Put the known information first and the new information at the end of the sentence.
- Keep subject and verb close together.
- Prefer active verbs to noun phrases with weak verbs: "was performed an analysis of" → "analysed".
- Vary sentence length, but keep most sentences under about 25 words.

## 4. Concision

Replace wordy phrases:
| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because |
| in order to | to |
| a large number of | many |
| at this point in time | now |
| has the ability to | can |
| it is important to note that | (delete, or state the point) |
| in the case of | for, in |
| with regard to | about, for |
| is in agreement with | agrees with |
| carried out an investigation of | investigated |
| a total of 12 samples | 12 samples |
| in the present study, it was found that | we found that / … was found |

Also cut: redundant pairs (each and every, final outcome, completely eliminate), empty openers (It is well known that, It should be mentioned that), and repetition of what a figure already shows.

## 5. Tense map by section

| Section | Main tense | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | past (what was done and found) | present for the gap, what the paper does, implications |
| Introduction | present (known facts) | past for specific studies; present perfect for research to date and open gaps |
| Literature review | present and past | present perfect for continuing lines of research |
| Methods | past | present for equations and for describing tables and figures |
| Results | past | present for what a figure or table shows; present for findings you treat as established |
| Discussion | present (meaning, generalization, literature) | past for your specific results; modals for interpretation |
| Conclusion | present and past | |

Keep the tense consistent within a passage; shift only for a reason.

**Present simple vs present continuous:** present simple states facts and accepted truths ("Copper catalysts deactivate by sintering"); present continuous describes temporary situations and is rare in papers ("Interest is growing" is acceptable for trends).

**Past simple vs present perfect:** past simple for completed studies at a definite time ("[12] measured …"); present perfect when the result still matters now or the period continues ("Several studies have shown …", "No study has yet …").

## 6. Active and passive voice

- Passive keeps the focus on the object and suits procedures: "The catalyst was reduced in H2 at [T]."
- Active is clearer when the actor matters: "We chose a finite-volume scheme because …"; "[12] showed that …".
- Avoid dangling constructions: "Using the model, the hot spot was predicted" (who used it?) → "The model predicted the hot spot" or "Using the model, we predicted …".

## 7. Signalling language (linking words)

| Function | Words |
|---|---|
| Addition | in addition, furthermore, moreover, also, likewise |
| Contrast | however, in contrast, whereas, while, conversely, on the other hand |
| Concession | although, even though, despite, nevertheless, nonetheless |
| Cause and result | because, since, owing to; therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, in particular |
| Sequence | first, second, subsequently, then, finally |
| Similarity | similarly, in the same way, likewise |
| Summary | in summary, overall, taken together, in short |
| Clarification | that is, in other words, namely |

Place "however" after the first phrase or at the start followed by a comma ("However, …"; "The model, however, …"). Do not join two clauses with "however" and a comma only.

## 8. Adverbs

- Place adverbs where they modify the right word: "only" before the word it limits ("The model only fails at high flow" vs "The model fails only at high flow").
- Mid-position adverbs (also, often, usually, generally) go before the main verb or after "be": "The error usually increases"; "The error is usually small".
- Avoid empty intensifiers (very, extremely, highly, quite) unless they add meaning; give the number instead.

## 9. Common problems for non-native writers

- **Compound nouns:** long noun stacks are ambiguous ("reactor wall temperature measurement error correction method"). Break them with prepositions.
- **Prepositions:** they change meaning; check them in a corpus or in good papers in your field (depend on, consist of, comparable to/with, effect of X on Y, agreement with).
- **Articles:** "the" for something specific or already mentioned; "a/an" for one of many; no article for general uncountable nouns and plurals ("Catalysts deactivate").
- **Countable and uncountable nouns:** "data" is usually plural in science ("the data were"); "information", "equipment", "research", "evidence" are uncountable (no plural, no "a").
- **"Respectively"** only for pairing two parallel lists: "The inlet and outlet temperatures were 500 and 530 K, respectively."
- **Which vs that:** "that" for defining clauses (no commas); ", which" for additional information (with commas).
- **Comparatives:** "higher than", "compared with" (to show differences), "compared to" (to liken); avoid "more higher".
- **Numbers:** see `results-language.md` §10.
- **Units:** "mass" not "weight"; SI symbols, never plural ("5 kg", not "5 kgs").

## 10. Terms, abbreviations and jargon

- Define each technical term and abbreviation at first use, in the abstract and again in the main text.
- Use abbreviations only for terms that appear often; too many abbreviations make text unreadable.
- Do not vary terms for elegance: if it is "selectivity" in Methods, it is "selectivity" everywhere.
- Explain jargon for readers from neighbouring fields; specialists are not your only readers.

## 11. Counterarguments

To strengthen a claim, raise the strongest objection and answer it:
1. Introduce it fairly: "It could be argued that …"; "One objection is …".
2. Answer with evidence: "However, the sensitivity analysis shows …".
3. Agree in part where it is valid: "This is true for …, but …".
4. Reaffirm the claim in its qualified form.

## 12. Punctuation reminders

- Comma after introductory phrases ("In contrast, …").
- Colon before a list or explanation that completes the sentence.
- Semicolon between closely related independent clauses, or in complex lists.
- En dash for ranges in references and between names (Peng–Robinson); hyphen in compound adjectives (fixed-bed reactor, two-dimensional model).
